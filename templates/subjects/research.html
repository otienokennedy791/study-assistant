1. Introduction to Nursing Research ＋
📌 Meaning and Purpose of Research

Meaning

Research is a systematic and organized process of collecting, analyzing and interpreting information in order to answer questions or solve problems.

Purpose of Nursing Research

  • Generate new knowledge.
  • Improve patient care.
  • Evaluate existing nursing interventions.
  • Identify health problems.
  • Improve healthcare policies and practices.
  • Support evidence-based nursing practice.
📌 Research Process

The research process consists of a series of connected steps used to answer a research question systematically.

  1. Identify the research problem.
  2. Review existing literature.
  3. Develop objectives and research questions.
  4. Select an appropriate research design.
  5. Identify the population and sample.
  6. Collect data.
  7. Analyze data.
  8. Interpret findings.
  9. Write and disseminate the research report.
📌 Types of Research
  • Quantitative research: Uses numerical data and statistical analysis.
  • Qualitative research: Explores experiences, perceptions and meanings.
  • Mixed-methods research: Combines quantitative and qualitative approaches.
  • Applied research: Addresses practical problems.
  • Basic research: Expands theoretical knowledge.
📌 Evidence-Based Practice

Evidence-based practice involves integrating the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values and preferences when making healthcare decisions.

Example: A nurse may use current research evidence together with clinical experience and the patient's preferences when selecting an appropriate intervention.
2. Research Problem and Background ＋
📌 Identifying a Research Problem

A research problem is an issue, gap in knowledge or situation that requires systematic investigation.

Sources of Research Problems

  • Clinical practice.
  • Previous research.
  • Community health problems.
  • Healthcare policies.
  • Professional observations.
  • Theories and academic literature.
📌 Problem Statement

A problem statement clearly describes the health or nursing issue being investigated, its importance and the gap that the research intends to address.

A good problem statement should:

  • Clearly identify the problem.
  • Provide relevant background information.
  • Show the magnitude or importance of the problem.
  • Identify the knowledge or practice gap.
  • Provide justification for conducting the study.
📌 Background of the Study

The background provides contextual information about the research problem. It moves from the broad situation to the specific problem being investigated.

A strong background may include relevant statistics, previous studies, current practice, geographical context and the specific gap that needs investigation.

📌 Significance of the Study

The significance explains who may benefit from the research and how the findings could contribute to nursing practice, education, policy, administration or future research.

  • Patients and communities.
  • Nurses and other healthcare professionals.
  • Healthcare institutions.
  • Policy makers.
  • Researchers and students.
3. Literature Review ＋
📌 Purpose of Literature Review

A literature review involves systematically examining published information related to the research topic.

  • Provides background knowledge.
  • Identifies gaps in knowledge.
  • Prevents unnecessary duplication.
  • Helps develop research questions.
  • Guides selection of research methods.
  • Provides a basis for interpreting findings.
📌 Search Strategy

A search strategy is a planned approach for finding relevant literature.

Important components

  • Identify keywords.
  • Use synonyms and related terms.
  • Use appropriate databases.
  • Apply inclusion and exclusion criteria.
  • Record useful references.
  • Assess the quality and relevance of sources.
📌 Sources of Literature
  • Peer-reviewed journal articles.
  • Books and textbooks.
  • Government publications.
  • Professional guidelines.
  • Research reports.
  • Theses and dissertations.
  • Reliable electronic databases.
Academic sources should be relevant, credible and as current as appropriate for the research question.
📌 Critical Appraisal and Synthesis

Critical appraisal involves judging the quality, relevance and credibility of evidence.

Synthesis involves bringing findings from different sources together to identify patterns, agreements, disagreements and gaps.

4. Research Objectives, Questions and Hypotheses ＋
📌 General and Specific Objectives

The general objective states the overall purpose of the study. Specific objectives break that purpose into measurable components.

Example: General objective: To assess factors associated with medication adherence among patients with hypertension.

Specific objectives should be clear, measurable, realistic and directly related to the research problem.

📌 Research Questions

Research questions are specific questions that the study intends to answer.

They should be clear, focused, researchable and consistent with the objectives.

📌 Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting a relationship or difference between variables.

  • Null hypothesis: States that there is no significant relationship or difference.
  • Alternative hypothesis: States that a relationship or difference exists.
📌 Variables

A variable is a characteristic or factor that can take different values.

  • Independent variable: The factor considered to influence another variable.
  • Dependent variable: The outcome being measured.
  • Confounding variable: A factor that may distort the relationship between variables.
📊 Conceptual Framework

A conceptual framework shows how important concepts or variables in a study are expected to relate to one another.

5. Research Designs ＋
📌 Quantitative Designs

Quantitative designs are used when researchers collect numerical data and analyze it statistically.

  • Descriptive studies.
  • Correlational studies.
  • Experimental studies.
  • Quasi-experimental studies.
  • Cross-sectional studies.
  • Cohort studies.
📌 Qualitative Designs

Qualitative research explores human experiences, meanings, perceptions and social processes.

  • Phenomenology.
  • Grounded theory.
  • Ethnography.
  • Case study.
  • Narrative research.
📌 Mixed Methods

Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches within one study.

It can provide both numerical measurements and detailed understanding of participants' experiences.

📌 Experimental vs Nonexperimental Research
Experimental Nonexperimental
Researcher actively introduces an intervention or manipulation. Researcher observes variables without manipulating them.
May include a control group. May use descriptive or correlational approaches.
Can be used to investigate cause-and-effect relationships. Usually describes or examines associations.

6. Sampling and Participants ＋
📌 Population and Sample

The population is the entire group of people or units that the researcher is interested in studying.

A sample is a smaller group selected from the population to participate in the study.

📌 Probability Sampling

In probability sampling, members of the population have a known chance of being selected.

  • Simple random sampling.
  • Systematic sampling.
  • Stratified sampling.
  • Cluster sampling.
  • Multistage sampling.
📌 Nonprobability Sampling
  • Convenience sampling.
  • Purposive sampling.
  • Quota sampling.
  • Snowball sampling.
  • Consecutive sampling.

Nonprobability sampling does not give every population member a known probability of selection.

📌 Sample Size

Sample size refers to the number of participants or units included in a study.

Factors affecting sample size include study design, research objectives, expected variability, desired precision, available resources and statistical requirements.

📌 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria describe characteristics participants must have to take part in the study.

Exclusion criteria describe characteristics that prevent otherwise eligible individuals from participating.

7. Data Collection Methods ＋
📌 Questionnaires

A questionnaire is a structured tool containing questions used to collect information from participants.

Questions may be open-ended, closed-ended or use rating scales such as Likert-type responses.

📌 Interviews

Interviews involve obtaining information directly from participants through verbal communication.

  • Structured interviews.
  • Semi-structured interviews.
  • Unstructured interviews.
📌 Observation

Observation involves systematically watching and recording behaviors, events or characteristics relevant to the study.

Observation can be structured or unstructured and may involve participant or nonparticipant observation.

📌 Records and Document Review

Researchers may collect information from existing documents such as patient records, registers, reports, policies and administrative databases.

Confidentiality and appropriate authorization must be considered when accessing health records.
📌 Validity and Reliability

Validity refers to whether a tool measures what it is intended to measure.

Reliability refers to the consistency of measurements obtained using a research instrument.

8. Data Analysis and Presentation ＋
📌 Quantitative Analysis

Quantitative data analysis involves organizing and analyzing numerical information using statistical methods.

  • Frequencies and percentages.
  • Measures of central tendency.
  • Measures of dispersion.
  • Tests of association.
  • Tests of differences.
  • Regression analysis where appropriate.
📌 Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative analysis involves identifying meaningful patterns, categories and themes in non-numerical data.

  1. Become familiar with the data.
  2. Code relevant information.
  3. Group related codes.
  4. Develop categories or themes.
  5. Review and interpret themes.
  6. Present findings using appropriate evidence.
📌 Tables and Charts

Tables and charts help researchers present findings clearly and make patterns easier to understand.

  • Frequency tables.
  • Bar charts.
  • Pie charts.
  • Histograms.
  • Line graphs.
📌 Interpretation of Findings

Interpretation involves explaining what the results mean in relation to the research questions, objectives, literature and theoretical framework.

Results should be interpreted objectively. Researchers should distinguish between what the data demonstrate and possible explanations or implications.

9. Research Ethics ＋
📌 Informed Consent

Informed consent is the process through which participants receive adequate information about a study and voluntarily agree to participate.

Information should generally include the purpose of the study, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation and the right to withdraw according to applicable requirements.

📌 Confidentiality and Anonymity

Confidentiality means protecting information provided by participants and limiting access to authorized persons.

Anonymity means that participant identity is not linked to the information collected, where the study design allows this.

📌 Beneficence and Nonmaleficence

Beneficence refers to promoting participant welfare and maximizing potential benefits.

Nonmaleficence means avoiding or minimizing unnecessary harm.

📌 Justice

Justice requires fair treatment and fair distribution of the burdens and potential benefits of research.

📌 Ethical Review

Research involving human participants generally requires appropriate ethical review before data collection begins, according to applicable institutional and regulatory requirements.

Ethical review considers issues such as risks, benefits, consent, privacy, confidentiality, participant selection and protection of vulnerable participants.

10. Research Proposal and Case Study Development ＋
📌 Research Proposal Structure

A research proposal is a formal document describing what the researcher intends to investigate and how the study will be conducted.

Common components

  1. Title.
  2. Introduction and background.
  3. Problem statement.
  4. Justification/significance.
  5. Objectives.
  6. Research questions or hypotheses.
  7. Literature review.
  8. Conceptual or theoretical framework.
  9. Research methodology.
  10. Ethical considerations.
  11. Work plan and budget where required.
  12. References.
  13. Appendices.
📌 Case Study Structure

A case study provides a detailed examination of an individual, family, group, organization, community or situation.

Common components

  • Introduction and case identification.
  • Relevant history and background.
  • Assessment or data collection.
  • Analysis of the case.
  • Problems or diagnoses identified.
  • Interventions or management.
  • Outcomes.
  • Discussion and lessons learned.
  • Conclusion and recommendations.
📌 Referencing and Academic Writing

Academic writing should be clear, organized, evidence-based and appropriately referenced.

  • Use credible academic sources.
  • Reference information appropriately.
  • Avoid plagiarism.
  • Use clear academic language.
  • Maintain logical flow between sections.
  • Follow the required referencing style consistently.
📌 Presentation and Defense

A proposal or case study presentation should communicate the important components of the work clearly and confidently.

Preparation should include:

  • Understanding the research problem.
  • Knowing the objectives and methodology.
  • Understanding the supporting literature.
  • Preparing clear presentation slides.
  • Practicing responses to possible questions.
  • Explaining methodological choices logically.
