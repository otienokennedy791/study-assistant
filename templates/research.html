Research Proposal & Case Study Development

This course develops the knowledge and practical skills needed to identify a research problem, formulate objectives and questions, review literature, select appropriate research methods, collect and analyze data, observe research ethics, and prepare a complete research proposal or case study.

Learning approach: Study each topic by working through the subtopics. Where a diagram is provided, use it to understand how the different concepts are connected. After studying, attempt the short-answer and long-answer questions and then practice the NURS 497 MCQs.

1. Introduction to Nursing Research

▼

1.1 Meaning of Research

Research is a systematic process of collecting, analyzing and interpreting information to answer a question, solve a problem or generate new knowledge.

Nursing research is systematic inquiry designed to develop knowledge relevant to nursing practice, education, administration and patient care.

1.2 Characteristics of Good Research

  • Systematic and organized.
  • Based on a clearly identified problem.
  • Uses appropriate methods.
  • Ethically conducted.
  • Evidence-based.
  • Can be analyzed and interpreted logically.
  • Results can contribute to knowledge or practice.

1.3 Importance of Research in Nursing

  • Improves quality and safety of patient care.
  • Supports evidence-based practice.
  • Identifies health problems and their causes.
  • Improves nursing procedures and interventions.
  • Supports development of nursing policies.
  • Contributes to professional development.
Example: A nurse may conduct research to determine whether a particular patient-education approach improves medication adherence among patients with hypertension.

1.4 Evidence-Based Nursing Practice

Evidence-based practice combines the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the patient's needs, preferences and values.

2. Identification of Research Problems

▼

2.1 Research Problem

A research problem is a situation, gap in knowledge, unanswered question or practical difficulty that requires systematic investigation.

Example: Low adherence to prescribed tuberculosis treatment in a particular community may become a research problem if the reasons for poor adherence are not adequately known.

2.2 Sources of Research Problems

  • Clinical practice.
  • Previous research.
  • Literature reviews.
  • Community health problems.
  • Nursing theories.
  • Personal professional observations.
  • Government and health reports.

2.3 Criteria for Selecting a Research Problem

  • Significance to health or nursing.
  • Feasibility.
  • Availability of participants.
  • Availability of time and resources.
  • Ethical acceptability.
  • Researcher's knowledge and skills.
  • Availability of literature.

2.4 Statement of the Problem

The problem statement clearly explains the issue being investigated, the affected population, the existing gap and why the problem deserves investigation.

A strong problem statement moves from the broader situation to the specific research gap.

3. Research Objectives, Questions and Hypotheses

▼

3.1 General Objective

The general objective states the main purpose of the research. It should directly correspond to the research problem.

Example: To determine factors associated with medication adherence among adult patients attending a medical outpatient clinic.

3.2 Specific Objectives

Specific objectives break the general objective into smaller measurable components.

Common action verbs include:

  • To determine
  • To assess
  • To describe
  • To identify
  • To compare
  • To examine
  • To establish

3.3 Research Questions

Research questions are questions that the study is designed to answer.

Example: What factors influence medication adherence among adult patients attending the clinic?

3.4 Research Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables.

Quantitative studies may use a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis.

4. Literature Review

▼

4.1 Meaning of Literature Review

A literature review is a critical examination and synthesis of existing knowledge related to a research topic.

4.2 Sources of Literature

  • Peer-reviewed journal articles.
  • Textbooks.
  • Government publications.
  • Professional guidelines.
  • Research reports.
  • Theses and dissertations.
  • Reliable electronic databases.

4.3 Steps in Literature Review

  1. Define the research topic.
  2. Develop search terms.
  3. Search relevant databases and sources.
  4. Screen relevant studies.
  5. Assess quality and relevance.
  6. Organize findings into themes.
  7. Synthesize the evidence.
  8. Identify gaps.
  9. Relate the gap to the proposed study.

4.4 Literature Gap

A literature gap is an area where existing evidence is insufficient, inconsistent, outdated or unavailable.

6. Population, Sampling and Sample Size

▼

6.1 Target Population

The target population is the entire group to which the researcher intends to generalize the findings.

6.2 Accessible Population

The accessible population is the portion of the target population that is available to the researcher.

6.3 Sampling

Sampling is the process of selecting a smaller group from a population to participate in a study.

6.4 Probability Sampling

  • Simple random sampling.
  • Systematic sampling.
  • Stratified sampling.
  • Cluster sampling.
  • Multistage sampling.

6.5 Non-Probability Sampling

  • Convenience sampling.
  • Purposive sampling.
  • Quota sampling.
  • Snowball sampling.

6.6 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria specify characteristics that make a person eligible to participate, while exclusion criteria specify characteristics that prevent participation.

7. Data Collection Methods and Research Instruments

▼

7.1 Primary and Secondary Data

Primary data are collected directly by the researcher for the current study.

Secondary data are obtained from information that already exists, such as records, reports and previous studies.

7.2 Data Collection Methods

  • Questionnaires.
  • Interviews.
  • Observation.
  • Focus group discussions.
  • Document review.
  • Physical measurements.

7.3 Research Instruments

A research instrument is a tool used to collect data. Examples include questionnaires, interview guides, observation checklists and data-extraction forms.

7.4 Validity and Reliability

Validity refers to whether an instrument measures what it is intended to measure.

Reliability refers to the consistency of measurements produced by an instrument.

8. Data Management, Analysis and Interpretation

▼

8.1 Data Management

Data management involves organizing, storing, protecting and preparing collected information for analysis.

  • Editing.
  • Coding.
  • Data entry.
  • Data cleaning.
  • Secure storage.
  • Confidentiality protection.

8.2 Quantitative Data Analysis

Quantitative data may be summarized using frequencies, percentages, measures of central tendency and measures of dispersion. Inferential statistics may be used to examine relationships or differences where appropriate.

8.3 Qualitative Data Analysis

Qualitative analysis commonly involves organizing data, coding meaningful sections, developing categories and identifying themes.

8.4 Interpretation of Findings

Interpretation involves explaining what the results mean in relation to the research questions, objectives, existing literature and the study context.

9. Research Ethics

▼

9.1 Importance of Research Ethics

Research ethics protect participants and promote responsible, trustworthy research.

9.2 Major Ethical Principles

  • Respect for persons.
  • Beneficence.
  • Non-maleficence.
  • Justice.
  • Confidentiality.
  • Privacy.
  • Voluntary participation.

9.3 Informed Consent

Informed consent means that participants receive adequate information about the study, understand what participation involves and voluntarily agree to take part.

9.4 Confidentiality and Anonymity

Confidentiality means protecting participant information from unauthorized disclosure. Anonymity means that the identity of a participant is not linked to the collected information.

9.5 Ethical Approval

Research involving human participants should undergo appropriate ethical review before data collection begins.

10.3 Case Study Development

A case study is an in-depth investigation of an individual, family, group, organization, community or specific situation.

In nursing, a case study may describe the patient's history, assessment findings, diagnosis, management, nursing interventions, outcomes and lessons learned.

  2. Presenting complaint.
  3. History.
  4. Physical assessment.
  5. Investigations.
  6. Medical diagnosis.
  7. Nursing diagnoses.
  8. Nursing management.
  9. Patient education.
  10. Evaluation and outcomes.
  11. Discussion.
  12. Conclusion and recommendations.

1. Define nursing research.

Nursing research is systematic inquiry conducted to generate knowledge that improves nursing practice, education, administration and patient outcomes.

2. State five characteristics of good research.

Good research should be systematic, ethical, relevant, evidence-based and methodologically appropriate.

3. Differentiate between a research problem and a research question.

A research problem is the issue or gap requiring investigation, while a research question is the specific question the study seeks to answer.

4. State five sources of research problems.

Clinical practice, previous research, literature reviews, community health problems and professional observations.

5. Differentiate between quantitative and qualitative research.

Quantitative research primarily uses numerical data to measure variables and examine patterns or relationships, while qualitative research explores experiences, meanings, perceptions and social processes using non-numerical data.

6. What is informed consent?

Informed consent is a voluntary agreement to participate in research after receiving adequate information about the study, its procedures, risks, benefits and the participant's rights.

7. Differentiate validity from reliability.

Validity concerns whether an instrument measures what it is intended to measure, while reliability concerns the consistency of the measurements it produces.

8. What is sampling?

Sampling is the process of selecting participants from a larger population to take part in a research study.

1. Explain the major steps involved in developing a research proposal.

Developing a research proposal begins with identifying a significant research problem. The researcher then develops a clear problem statement explaining the issue and the knowledge gap.

The researcher formulates a general objective and specific objectives, followed by research questions or hypotheses where appropriate.

A literature review is then conducted to establish what is already known, identify gaps and provide the background for the study.

The researcher develops an appropriate methodology, including the study design, population, sampling procedure, sample size, data collection instruments, data management and analysis methods.

Ethical considerations are addressed, including informed consent, confidentiality, privacy, participant safety and ethical approval.

Finally, the proposal includes a work plan, budget, references and relevant appendices before submission for review and approval.

2. Discuss the importance of research in nursing practice.

Research provides evidence that helps nurses make informed clinical decisions instead of relying only on tradition or personal experience.

It helps identify patient needs, evaluate nursing interventions and improve patient safety and quality of care.

Research also contributes to development of nursing policies, education and professional standards.

Through research, nurses can identify gaps in existing knowledge and develop interventions that are appropriate for particular populations and healthcare settings.

Therefore, nursing research strengthens evidence-based practice and contributes to better health outcomes.

3. Discuss the major ethical principles that should guide research involving human participants.

Respect for persons requires researchers to recognize the autonomy and rights of participants and obtain informed consent.

Beneficence requires researchers to maximize potential benefits, while non-maleficence requires them to minimize potential harm.

Justice requires fair selection and treatment of research participants.

Confidentiality and privacy require researchers to protect participant information and prevent unauthorized disclosure.

Ethical review should also be obtained where required before beginning research involving human participants.
