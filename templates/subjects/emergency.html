First Aid, Trauma & Emergency Nursing

This section introduces the principles and nursing management of patients experiencing acute illness, injury and life-threatening emergencies.

Study approach: Open each topic, then click individual subtopics to study them. Relevant diagrams are included where they help explain a concept.
1. Principles of First Aid and Emergency Care ＋
Definition and Objectives of First Aid ＋

Definition

First aid is the immediate and temporary care given to a person who has been injured or suddenly becomes ill before definitive medical treatment is available.

Objectives

  • Preserve life.
  • Prevent the condition from becoming worse.
  • Promote recovery.
  • Relieve pain and anxiety.
  • Arrange appropriate further medical care.
Important: First aid does not replace definitive medical treatment.
Principles of Emergency Care ＋
  1. Ensure the scene is safe.
  2. Use appropriate personal protective equipment.
  3. Assess the patient's responsiveness.
  4. Assess airway, breathing and circulation.
  5. Identify life-threatening problems first.
  6. Call for appropriate assistance.
  7. Provide immediate interventions.
  8. Continuously reassess the patient.
Emergency nursing prioritizes conditions that threaten life, particularly problems involving airway, breathing and circulation.
Primary and Secondary Survey ＋

Primary Survey

The primary survey rapidly identifies and treats immediate threats to life.

  • A — Airway: Check and maintain airway.
  • B — Breathing: Assess breathing and oxygenation.
  • C — Circulation: Assess pulse and control major bleeding.
  • D — Disability: Assess neurological status.
  • E — Exposure: Expose the patient as necessary while preventing heat loss.

Secondary Survey

A systematic head-to-toe assessment performed after immediate life-threatening problems have been addressed.

2. Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Basic Life Support ＋
Recognition of Cardiac Arrest ＋

Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart suddenly stops producing effective circulation.

Common findings

  • Unresponsiveness.
  • Absent or abnormal breathing.
  • No signs of normal circulation.
Activate the emergency response system and begin appropriate resuscitation without unnecessary delay.
Adult Basic Life Support Sequence ＋
  1. Ensure scene safety.
  2. Check responsiveness.
  3. Call for help and activate emergency response.
  4. Assess breathing and circulation according to the applicable protocol.
  5. Begin chest compressions when indicated.
  6. Provide rescue breaths when trained and appropriate.
  7. Use an automated external defibrillator when available and indicated.
  8. Continue until the patient shows signs of recovery or advanced help takes over.

Automated External Defibrillator ＋

An automated external defibrillator (AED) is a device that analyzes the cardiac rhythm and, when appropriate, delivers an electrical shock.

  • Turn on the AED.
  • Attach the pads correctly.
  • Allow the device to analyze the rhythm.
  • Ensure nobody is touching the patient during analysis/shock.
  • Follow the device prompts.
  • Resume CPR when instructed.
3. Shock and Circulatory Emergencies ＋
Definition and Types of Shock ＋

Shock is a state of inadequate tissue perfusion in which oxygen and nutrients delivered to tissues are insufficient for cellular needs.

Major types

  • Hypovolemic shock.
  • Cardiogenic shock.
  • Distributive shock.
  • Obstructive shock.
Hypovolemic Shock ＋

Hypovolemic shock occurs when there is significant loss of circulating blood or body fluid.

Causes

  • Severe external or internal bleeding.
  • Severe burns.
  • Profuse vomiting or diarrhea.
  • Major fluid losses.

Possible manifestations

  • Rapid pulse.
  • Low blood pressure, especially as shock progresses.
  • Cool or clammy skin.
  • Reduced urine output.
  • Anxiety, confusion or reduced consciousness.
Nursing Management of Shock ＋
  1. Rapidly assess ABCs.
  2. Call for emergency assistance.
  3. Control obvious external bleeding.
  4. Maintain airway and support breathing.
  5. Administer oxygen when clinically indicated.
  6. Monitor vital signs frequently.
  7. Establish and maintain appropriate IV access.
  8. Administer prescribed fluids, blood products or medications.
  9. Monitor urine output.
  10. Keep the patient warm while avoiding overheating.
  11. Continuously reassess response to treatment.

4. Bleeding, Wounds and Hemorrhage ＋
Types of Bleeding ＋

Arterial bleeding

Blood from an artery is generally bright red and may be expelled forcefully or in spurts.

Venous bleeding

Venous bleeding is generally darker and may flow steadily.

Capillary bleeding

Capillary bleeding usually involves slow oozing from small vessels.

Control of External Hemorrhage ＋
  1. Ensure personal and scene safety.
  2. Use gloves and appropriate protective equipment.
  3. Apply direct pressure where appropriate.
  4. Use appropriate dressings or haemostatic measures according to protocol.
  5. Consider a tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding when indicated and trained to use one.
  6. Monitor the patient for signs of shock.
  7. Arrange urgent definitive care.
Major hemorrhage can become life-threatening rapidly. Immediate control of bleeding is a priority.
Wound Assessment and Care ＋

Assess for:

  • Location and size.
  • Depth.
  • Bleeding.
  • Contamination.
  • Foreign bodies.
  • Damage to nerves, vessels, tendons or bones.
  • Signs of infection.
5. Fractures, Dislocations and Musculoskeletal Trauma ＋
Types of Fractures ＋
  • Closed fracture.
  • Open fracture.
  • Complete fracture.
  • Incomplete fracture.
  • Transverse fracture.
  • Oblique fracture.
  • Spiral fracture.
  • Comminuted fracture.
Signs and Symptoms of Fractures ＋
  • Pain and tenderness.
  • Swelling.
  • Deformity.
  • Reduced movement.
  • Abnormal mobility.
  • Bruising.
  • Crepitus may occur.

Neurovascular status distal to the injury should also be assessed.

First Aid and Nursing Care of Fractures ＋
  1. Assess ABCs first.
  2. Control life-threatening bleeding.
  3. Immobilize the injured area.
  4. Assess distal circulation, sensation and movement.
  5. Provide pain management as prescribed.
  6. Monitor for complications.
  7. Arrange imaging and definitive treatment.

6. Head Injury and Spinal Trauma ＋
Assessment of Head Injury ＋

Assess:

  • Level of consciousness.
  • Pupil size and reaction.
  • Motor function.
  • Speech and orientation.
  • Headache and vomiting.
  • Seizures.
  • Evidence of skull or facial injury.
A deterioration in neurological status is an emergency and requires immediate reassessment and escalation.
Spinal Cord Injury ＋

Spinal trauma can damage the spinal cord and may result in temporary or permanent neurological impairment.

Possible manifestations

  • Neck or back pain.
  • Weakness or paralysis.
  • Loss or alteration of sensation.
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Respiratory impairment in high spinal injuries.

Patients with suspected spinal injury should be handled using appropriate spinal precautions according to current emergency protocols.

Glasgow Coma Scale ＋

The Glasgow Coma Scale is used to describe a patient's level of consciousness using three components.

  • Eye opening.
  • Verbal response.
  • Motor response.
Record the individual component scores as well as the total score to communicate neurological changes accurately.
7. Burns and Thermal Injuries ＋
Types and Causes of Burns ＋
  • Thermal burns.
  • Chemical burns.
  • Electrical burns.
  • Radiation burns.
  • Inhalation injury associated with fire or smoke exposure.
Assessment of Burn Severity ＋

Burn assessment considers depth, extent, location, mechanism and associated injuries.

Important considerations

  • Percentage of total body surface area involved.
  • Depth of burn.
  • Face, airway, hands, feet, genitalia and major joint involvement.
  • Electrical or chemical mechanism.
  • Evidence of inhalation injury.
  • Age and general health of the patient.
📊 Diagram: Burn Assessment Concept
Emergency Nursing Care of Burns ＋
  1. Ensure scene safety.
  2. Stop the burning process when safe to do so.
  3. Assess airway and breathing.
  4. Remove constricting items early when appropriate.
  5. Cover burns appropriately.
  6. Prevent hypothermia.
  7. Assess circulation and fluid losses.
  8. Administer prescribed analgesia and fluids.
  9. Monitor urine output and vital signs.
  10. Arrange referral for appropriate burn care.
8. Poisoning and Chemical Emergencies ＋
Routes of Poison Exposure ＋
  • Ingestion.
  • Inhalation.
  • Skin exposure.
  • Eye exposure.
  • Injection or bites/stings.
Initial Management of Poisoning ＋
  1. Ensure scene safety.
  2. Protect healthcare workers from exposure.
  3. Assess ABCs.
  4. Remove the patient from ongoing exposure when safe.
  5. Identify the substance if possible.
  6. Do not induce vomiting unless specifically directed by an appropriate poison-management protocol.
  7. Provide supportive treatment.
  8. Use specific antidotes when indicated and prescribed.
  9. Monitor for deterioration.
Management depends on the substance, dose, route and time of exposure. Always follow the appropriate poison-control or emergency protocol.
Chemical Eye and Skin Exposure ＋

Chemical exposure to the eyes or skin can cause serious tissue injury. Immediate decontamination is often critical.

  • Protect yourself from the chemical.
  • Remove contaminated clothing when appropriate.
  • Irrigate exposed areas according to the relevant protocol.
  • Continue assessment and arrange urgent medical evaluation.
9. Respiratory Emergencies ＋
Airway Obstruction ＋

Airway obstruction prevents adequate movement of air into and out of the lungs and can rapidly become fatal.

Possible signs

  • Difficulty breathing.
  • Inability to speak normally.
  • Weak or absent cough.
  • Noisy breathing.
  • Cyanosis in severe obstruction.
  • Loss of consciousness.
Asthma Emergency ＋

A severe asthma attack may produce marked respiratory distress and requires urgent assessment and treatment.

Nursing priorities

  • Assess airway and breathing.
  • Monitor respiratory rate and oxygen saturation.
  • Position the patient to optimize breathing.
  • Administer prescribed bronchodilator therapy.
  • Administer oxygen when indicated.
  • Monitor response to treatment.
  • Escalate care if the patient deteriorates.
Drowning and Near-Drowning ＋

Drowning is a respiratory impairment resulting from submersion or immersion in liquid.

  • Ensure rescuer safety.
  • Remove the person from the water safely.
  • Assess responsiveness and breathing.
  • Start resuscitation when indicated.
  • Provide oxygen and supportive care when appropriate.
  • Monitor for respiratory complications.
10. Emergency Triage and Disaster Nursing ＋
Definition and Purpose of Triage ＋

Triage is the process of rapidly assessing patients and prioritizing them according to urgency and available resources.

Objectives

  • Identify patients requiring immediate intervention.
  • Use available resources efficiently.
  • Reduce preventable deaths and complications.
  • Direct patients to appropriate levels of care.
Triage Categories ＋

Specific triage categories and colors vary by system and institution, but commonly represent different levels of urgency.

  • Immediate: Requires urgent life-saving intervention.
  • Urgent: Serious condition requiring prompt treatment.
  • Delayed: Can safely wait for treatment.
  • Minimal: Minor injuries or conditions.
📊 Diagram: Triage Concept
Disaster Nursing ＋

Disaster nursing involves providing nursing care during events that overwhelm or threaten to overwhelm available resources.

Nurse responsibilities

  • Participate in disaster preparedness.
  • Perform triage.
  • Provide emergency care.
  • Coordinate with the multidisciplinary team.
  • Maintain accurate documentation.
  • Support infection prevention and control.
  • Provide psychological support.
  • Participate in recovery and rehabilitation.

1. Discuss the principles of emergency nursing care.

Begin with scene safety and appropriate personal protection. Rapidly assess the patient's airway, breathing and circulation and immediately address life-threatening problems. Assess neurological status, expose the patient when necessary while preventing hypothermia, obtain a focused history, monitor vital signs and initiate appropriate interventions. The patient should be continuously reassessed because their condition can change rapidly.

2. Discuss the nursing management of a patient in hypovolemic shock.

Nursing management begins with rapid assessment of airway, breathing and circulation. The nurse should identify and control external bleeding, provide oxygen when indicated, establish appropriate IV access, monitor vital signs and administer prescribed fluids or blood products. Urine output should be monitored as an indicator of perfusion. The nurse should maintain body temperature, monitor mental status, provide prescribed analgesia where appropriate, document interventions and continuously reassess the response to treatment.

3. Discuss the emergency management of a patient with major trauma.

Management begins with scene safety and a rapid primary survey. Airway patency, breathing and circulation are assessed and treated immediately. Major hemorrhage is controlled, neurological status is assessed and appropriate spinal precautions are maintained when indicated. A secondary survey follows stabilization and includes a systematic head-to-toe examination, history, vital signs and appropriate investigations. Continuous monitoring and early referral or escalation are essential.

4. Discuss the nursing management of a patient with severe burns.

Nursing care includes ensuring safety, stopping the burning process, assessing the airway and breathing, identifying possible inhalation injury, covering burns appropriately, preventing hypothermia, monitoring circulation and fluid balance, administering prescribed fluids and analgesics, monitoring urine output and preventing infection. The nurse also provides psychological support and prepares the patient for definitive burn management.

5. Explain the role of a nurse in disaster management.

The nurse participates in disaster preparedness, emergency response, triage, treatment and rehabilitation. During a disaster, the nurse may identify patients according to urgency, provide life-saving interventions, coordinate with other professionals, maintain documentation, support infection prevention and provide psychological support. After the event, nurses may participate in recovery, rehabilitation, evaluation and improvement of future disaster plans.

6. Discuss the assessment and management of a patient with a head injury.

Assessment includes airway and breathing, circulation, level of consciousness, pupils, motor function, speech, orientation and signs of skull or neurological injury. The Glasgow Coma Scale can be used to document neurological status. The patient should be monitored for deterioration, vomiting, seizures and changes in consciousness. Appropriate airway support, oxygen when indicated, spinal precautions when necessary, monitoring and urgent medical evaluation are essential.
