Welcome to Theatre Nursing

Theatre nursing focuses on the care of patients before, during and immediately after surgical procedures. A theatre nurse must understand surgical asepsis, patient safety, surgical instruments, positioning, anaesthesia, surgical counts, infection prevention and postoperative complications.

This study section is organized from topics → subtopics → clinical applications so that you can study each area systematically.

1. Introduction to Theatre and Perioperative Nursing ▼

Meaning of Theatre Nursing

Theatre nursing is the specialized nursing practice concerned with preparing and caring for patients undergoing surgical procedures. It includes preoperative, intraoperative and immediate postoperative care.

The theatre nurse protects the patient from avoidable harm while supporting the surgical team and maintaining a safe, sterile environment.

  • Preoperative phase: begins when surgery is planned and continues until transfer to the operating theatre.
  • Intraoperative phase: begins when the patient enters the operating theatre and ends when the patient is transferred to recovery.
  • Postoperative phase: begins in recovery and continues through the patient's recovery from surgery.

Roles of the Theatre Nurse

  • Prepare the patient physically and psychologically.
  • Maintain surgical asepsis.
  • Prepare instruments and equipment.
  • Assist with patient positioning.
  • Perform surgical counts.
  • Monitor patient safety.
  • Document theatre activities.
  • Communicate effectively with the multidisciplinary team.
Clinical example: Before an abdominal operation, the theatre nurse confirms the patient's identity, procedure, consent, allergies, surgical site and required equipment before the operation begins.
2. Operating Theatre Environment and Zones ▼

Theatre Environment

The operating theatre must be designed and managed to minimize contamination and support safe surgical procedures.

  • Good ventilation and air control.
  • Appropriate temperature and humidity.
  • Adequate lighting.
  • Easy-to-clean surfaces.
  • Proper waste disposal.
  • Controlled movement of personnel.

The Three Theatre Zones

📊 Diagram: Operating Theatre Zones
Unrestricted Zone

Reception and general areas
Semi-restricted Zone

Clean corridors and preparation areas
Restricted Zone

Operating rooms and sterile areas

Movement should progress from less controlled areas toward more controlled areas. Appropriate theatre clothing is required according to the zone.

Theatre Traffic Control

Excessive movement increases the risk of contamination. Theatre staff should limit unnecessary entry and exit from operating rooms.

Important: Good theatre discipline means minimizing unnecessary movement, maintaining appropriate attire and keeping doors closed whenever possible during surgery.
3. Preoperative Assessment and Preparation ▼

Preoperative Assessment

Preoperative assessment identifies conditions that may increase surgical or anaesthetic risk and provides baseline information for comparison after surgery.

  • Patient identity and demographic information.
  • Medical and surgical history.
  • Current medications.
  • Allergies.
  • Vital signs.
  • Airway assessment.
  • Nutritional and hydration status.
  • Laboratory and diagnostic results.
  • Psychological state.

Physical Preparation

  • Ensure required investigations are completed.
  • Follow prescribed fasting instructions.
  • Prepare the surgical site according to protocol.
  • Remove jewellery and other prohibited items.
  • Ensure appropriate identification.
  • Administer prescribed preoperative medications.
  • Ensure documentation is complete.

Psychological Preparation

Surgery can produce fear and anxiety. The nurse should provide accurate information, encourage questions and use therapeutic communication.

Clinical example: A patient scheduled for surgery may be worried about anaesthesia. The nurse listens to the concern, provides appropriate information within the nursing role and refers questions requiring the surgeon or anaesthetist to the appropriate professional.

4. Surgical Asepsis and Sterile Technique ▼

Medical and Surgical Asepsis

Medical asepsis reduces the number and spread of microorganisms, while surgical asepsis aims to maintain an environment free from microorganisms during invasive procedures.

Principles of Sterile Technique

📊 Diagram: Sterile Field Principles
Sterile touches sterile
Keep sterile field visible
Avoid reaching over sterile field
Keep sterile objects above waist level
Do not turn away from sterile field
Replace contaminated items
  • Only sterile items should touch sterile items.
  • Moisture can contaminate a sterile field through strike-through.
  • The outer edge of a sterile package is considered contaminated.
  • Sterile personnel must maintain appropriate sterile attire.
  • Any doubt about sterility should be treated as contamination.

Surgical Scrub, Gowning and Gloving

Surgical hand preparation reduces microorganisms on the hands before participation in sterile procedures. Sterile gowns and gloves are then applied using techniques designed to prevent contamination.

Clinical example: If a sterile glove touches a non-sterile surface, it is considered contaminated and must be replaced according to theatre protocol.
5. Surgical Instruments, Equipment and Supplies ▼

Common Categories of Surgical Instruments

  • Cutting and dissecting: scalpels and scissors.
  • Grasping and holding: forceps and tissue-holding instruments.
  • Clamping: haemostatic clamps.
  • Retracting: retractors.
  • Suturing: needle holders and related instruments.
  • Suction: suction devices and tubing.

Preparation of Instruments

Instruments must be cleaned, inspected, packaged, sterilized or high-level disinfected as appropriate, stored correctly and checked before use.

  • Check instrument integrity.
  • Ensure instruments are clean.
  • Check packaging and sterility indicators.
  • Arrange instruments systematically.
  • Ensure required equipment is functioning.

Instrument Handling

Safe instrument handling prevents injury, tissue trauma and contamination. Sharp instruments should be passed carefully and disposed of in appropriate sharps containers.

6. Intraoperative Nursing Care ▼

Patient Identification and Safety

The nurse confirms the patient's identity and procedure and supports the use of standardized surgical safety checks before incision.

Patient Positioning

Positioning provides surgical access while protecting the patient from pressure injuries, nerve damage, respiratory compromise and musculoskeletal injury.

  • Supine position.
  • Prone position.
  • Lateral position.
  • Trendelenburg position.
  • Lithotomy position.
Important: Padding should be used appropriately over pressure points, and the patient should be secured without compromising circulation or respiration.

Intraoperative Monitoring

  • Vital signs.
  • Oxygenation.
  • Blood loss.
  • Fluid balance.
  • Temperature.
  • Patient positioning and pressure areas.
  • Response to anaesthesia.

Role of Scrub and Circulating Nurses

The scrub nurse works within the sterile field and handles instruments and sterile supplies.

The circulating nurse works outside the sterile field, coordinates theatre activities, obtains additional supplies, documents events and supports patient safety.

7. Surgical Counts and Patient Safety ▼

Purpose of Surgical Counts

Surgical counts help prevent retained surgical items. Counts may include instruments, needles, sponges, swabs and other items according to institutional policy and the procedure being performed.

Surgical Count Process

📊 Diagram: Surgical Count Process
1. Initial count before the procedure.
2. Count during the procedure as required.
3. Closing count before wound closure according to protocol.
4. Final count before the patient leaves theatre.
5. Document the count and communicate discrepancies immediately.

Management of an Incorrect Count

If a count does not reconcile, the team must immediately follow institutional policy. The search should include the sterile field, instruments, waste, floor and other appropriate locations. Imaging or other escalation may be required according to the clinical situation and local policy.

Never ignore a count discrepancy. It is a patient-safety issue and must be communicated and managed immediately.
8. Anaesthesia and Immediate Recovery / PACU ▼

Types of Anaesthesia

  • General anaesthesia: produces a controlled state of unconsciousness.
  • Regional anaesthesia: blocks sensation in a larger region of the body.
  • Local anaesthesia: produces loss of sensation in a limited area.
  • Sedation: produces varying levels of reduced anxiety and awareness.

Responsibilities of the Nurse During Anaesthesia

  • Assist with preparation and monitoring.
  • Ensure appropriate equipment is available.
  • Monitor patient observations.
  • Maintain airway and safety according to role and competence.
  • Observe for complications.
  • Document relevant events.

Recovery Room / PACU

The post-anaesthesia care unit provides close monitoring while the patient recovers from anaesthesia and surgery.

  • Pulmonary embolism.
  • Urinary retention.

10. Infection Prevention, Documentation and Theatre Quality Improvement ▼

Infection Prevention

  • Hand hygiene.
  • Appropriate personal protective equipment.
  • Sterile technique.
  • Safe instrument processing.
  • Environmental cleaning.
  • Safe sharps disposal.
  • Appropriate waste segregation.
  • Antimicrobial stewardship according to policy.

Theatre Documentation

Accurate documentation supports continuity of care, patient safety, accountability and quality improvement.

  • Patient identification.
  • Procedure performed.
  • Personnel involved.
  • Patient positioning.
  • Counts.
  • Specimens.
  • Medications and fluids as applicable.
  • Patient condition and transfer.
  • Unexpected events and interventions.

1. Discuss the role of a theatre nurse in the perioperative care of a patient.

Introduction: The theatre nurse plays an important role in ensuring safe, coordinated and patient-centred surgical care.

Preoperative role: The nurse confirms patient identity, reviews relevant information, checks documentation and consent, assesses the patient's condition, prepares the patient physically and psychologically, and ensures required equipment and supplies are available.

Intraoperative role: The nurse maintains surgical asepsis, assists with positioning, performs surgical counts, handles instruments and supplies appropriately, monitors patient safety and communicates with the surgical team.

Postoperative role: The nurse supports safe transfer to recovery, communicates relevant patient information, monitors the patient's immediate condition and documents care.

Conclusion: Effective theatre nursing reduces preventable complications and promotes safe surgical outcomes.

2. Explain the principles of surgical asepsis and their importance in theatre nursing.

Surgical asepsis aims to prevent contamination during invasive procedures. Important principles include appropriate hand preparation, sterile attire, correct handling of sterile equipment, maintenance of the sterile field, controlled movement and replacement of contaminated items.

These principles are important because contamination can introduce microorganisms into surgical wounds and increase the risk of healthcare- associated infections and poor surgical outcomes.

The theatre nurse therefore continuously observes the sterile field and takes immediate corrective action when contamination occurs.

3. Discuss the management of a patient during the intraoperative period.

Intraoperative management includes confirming patient identity and procedure, ensuring appropriate positioning, maintaining asepsis, monitoring vital signs and physiological status, preventing pressure and nerve injuries, maintaining accurate surgical counts and supporting communication among the surgical team.

The nurse also ensures that instruments and equipment are available and functioning, assists with specimen management and maintains accurate documentation.

Patient safety remains the central priority throughout the operation.

4. Explain the importance of surgical counts and describe the actions taken when a count is incorrect.

Surgical counts are a major patient-safety measure designed to prevent retained instruments, needles, sponges or other surgical items.

Counts are performed at designated stages according to institutional policy. If a discrepancy occurs, the team should be informed immediately. A systematic search should be performed according to local policy, including the sterile field and other relevant locations. The discrepancy and actions taken should be documented and escalated appropriately.

The count must never simply be ignored because an unresolved discrepancy may expose the patient to serious harm.

5. Discuss postoperative complications and the role of the nurse in their prevention and early recognition.

Common postoperative complications include haemorrhage, respiratory compromise, infection, shock, thromboembolic events, urinary retention, nausea and vomiting, and wound complications.

The nurse prevents complications through appropriate monitoring, infection prevention, pain management, safe positioning, early mobilization where appropriate, fluid management and adherence to prescribed treatment.

Early recognition involves monitoring vital signs, respiratory status, consciousness, wound condition, drainage, urine output, pain and other relevant findings.

Any deterioration should be assessed promptly, appropriate interventions initiated within the nurse's scope of practice, the responsible clinical team notified and the patient's response documented.
