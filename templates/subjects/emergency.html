1. Principles of First Aid and Emergency Nursing

First aid is the immediate care provided to an injured or suddenly ill person before definitive medical treatment is available.

  • Ensure scene safety before approaching the patient.
  • Assess the patient systematically.
  • Call for appropriate emergency assistance.
  • Prioritize life-threatening problems.
  • Prevent further injury.
  • Provide reassurance and appropriate psychological support.
  • Document and communicate important findings.
2. Triage and Emergency Assessment

Triage is the process of rapidly assessing patients and prioritizing care according to urgency.

  • Airway and breathing problems receive immediate attention.
  • Severe circulation problems and major bleeding are priorities.
  • Level of consciousness is assessed.
  • Vital signs and relevant history are obtained.
  • Patients are reassessed because their condition can change.

A systematic primary survey commonly follows ABCDE: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability and Exposure.

3. Basic Life Support and CPR
  • Recognize cardiac arrest and activate the emergency response system.
  • Begin high-quality CPR according to current guidelines.
  • Provide chest compressions with appropriate rate and depth according to current resuscitation guidance.
  • Use an automated external defibrillator (AED) as soon as available when indicated.
  • Minimize unnecessary interruptions in chest compressions.
  • Continue reassessment and follow current resuscitation protocols.

Note: In actual emergencies, follow current local CPR and resuscitation guidelines and trained responder protocols.

4. Shock

Shock is a state of inadequate tissue perfusion resulting in insufficient oxygen delivery to tissues.

Major types

  • Hypovolemic shock
  • Cardiogenic shock
  • Distributive shock
  • Obstructive shock

Possible signs

  • Hypotension
  • Tachycardia
  • Altered mental status
  • Cool or clammy skin in many forms of shock
  • Reduced urine output
  • Weak peripheral pulses
5. Hemorrhage and Bleeding Control
  • Major external bleeding is a life-threatening emergency.
  • Apply appropriate direct pressure to external bleeding when possible.
  • Use appropriate wound dressings and bleeding-control measures.
  • Monitor for signs of shock.
  • Internal bleeding may present with pallor, weakness, abdominal or chest symptoms, altered consciousness or shock.
  • Rapid transport and definitive medical management may be required.
6. Wounds and Soft Tissue Injuries

Common wounds include abrasions, lacerations, punctures and avulsions.

  • Assess the wound and mechanism of injury.
  • Control bleeding.
  • Protect the wound from contamination.
  • Assess for foreign bodies and deeper injury.
  • Consider tetanus prevention according to clinical assessment and guidelines.
  • Monitor for infection.
7. Burns

Burns may be caused by thermal, chemical, electrical or other mechanisms.

  • Ensure the source of injury is made safe.
  • Assess airway and breathing in significant burns.
  • Assess burn depth and extent.
  • Consider the location of the burn, especially face, hands, feet, genitalia and major joints.
  • Prevent hypothermia.
  • Provide appropriate pain management.
  • Patients with significant burns may require specialized burn-center care.
8. Fractures, Dislocations and Splinting
  • A fracture is a break in the continuity of a bone.
  • A dislocation occurs when a joint surface is displaced from its normal position.
  • Assess circulation, sensation and movement distal to an injury.
  • Immobilize suspected fractures appropriately.
  • Avoid unnecessary movement.
  • Monitor for neurovascular compromise.
  • Provide pain management according to the treatment plan.
9. Head Injury and Traumatic Brain Injury
  • Head injuries may range from mild concussion to severe traumatic brain injury.
  • Assess level of consciousness and neurological status.
  • Monitor pupils and neurological changes.
  • Repeated vomiting, worsening headache, seizures or deteriorating consciousness are concerning findings.
  • Protect the cervical spine when significant trauma is suspected according to current trauma protocols.
  • Rapid medical assessment is required for significant head injury.
10. Spinal and Spinal Cord Injuries
  • Spinal injury may result in neurological impairment.
  • Suspect spinal injury based on mechanism and clinical findings.
  • Maintain appropriate spinal precautions according to current trauma protocols.
  • Assess motor and sensory function.
  • Monitor respiratory function in high spinal injuries.
  • Prevent secondary injury during movement and transfer.
11. Chest and Abdominal Trauma

Chest trauma

  • Assess airway and breathing immediately.
  • Monitor respiratory rate, oxygenation and chest movement.
  • Serious injuries may include pneumothorax, hemothorax and cardiac injury.

Abdominal trauma

  • Internal bleeding may occur without obvious external bleeding.
  • Assess pain, abdominal findings and signs of shock.
  • Urgent medical evaluation may be required.
12. Poisoning and Overdose
  • Identify the substance when possible without delaying emergency care.
  • Assess airway, breathing, circulation and level of consciousness.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless specifically directed by an appropriate poison-control or medical professional.
  • Bring the container or relevant information when safe and available.
  • Monitor vital signs and neurological status.
  • Follow local poison-control and emergency protocols.
13. Environmental and Temperature Emergencies

Heat-related illness

  • May include heat exhaustion and heat stroke.
  • Heat stroke is a medical emergency.
  • Rapid cooling and emergency medical care are required.

Hypothermia

  • Occurs when body temperature falls abnormally low.
  • Remove the patient from the cold environment.
  • Handle severely hypothermic patients carefully.
  • Provide appropriate rewarming and medical care.
14. Disaster and Mass Casualty Management
  • Disaster nursing requires organization and rapid prioritization.
  • Scene safety is essential.
  • Triage helps allocate limited resources.
  • Communication and coordination are important.
  • Documentation supports continuity of care.
  • Healthcare workers should work within established disaster plans.
15. Emergency Nursing and Patient Stabilization
  • Rapid assessment and reassessment are essential.
  • Identify and treat immediate life threats.
  • Monitor vital signs and response to interventions.
  • Provide appropriate oxygen therapy when indicated.
  • Establish access and prepare prescribed emergency medications and fluids.
  • Maintain accurate documentation.
  • Communicate changes promptly to the emergency team.

ABCDE Primary Survey
  • A — Airway: Check whether the airway is open and protected.
  • B — Breathing: Assess breathing, respiratory effort and oxygenation.
  • C — Circulation: Assess circulation and control life-threatening bleeding.
  • D — Disability: Assess neurological status and level of consciousness.
  • E — Exposure: Examine appropriately while preventing unnecessary heat loss.
Signs of Shock
  • Rapid pulse
  • Low blood pressure in many cases
  • Altered mental status
  • Reduced urine output
  • Weak peripheral pulses
  • Cool or clammy skin in many forms
  • Rapid breathing
Emergency Nurse Responsibilities
  • Rapid assessment
  • Triage
  • Monitoring
  • First aid and emergency interventions within scope
  • Medication administration as prescribed
  • Patient and family communication
  • Documentation
  • Reassessment and escalation of deterioration

Long-Answer Questions

Use these for examination practice.

1. Discuss the ABCDE approach to emergency assessment.

The ABCDE approach provides a systematic method of identifying and managing life-threatening problems.

Airway: The nurse assesses whether the airway is open and protected and identifies obstruction or other threats.

Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, effort, chest movement and oxygenation. Immediate treatment is provided for life-threatening breathing problems.

Circulation: Assess pulse and perfusion and identify and control major bleeding. Circulatory support is provided according to the patient's condition.

Disability: Assess level of consciousness and neurological status, including pupils and blood glucose when appropriate.

Exposure: Examine the patient sufficiently to identify injuries while maintaining privacy and preventing hypothermia.

The patient should be reassessed repeatedly because the clinical condition can change.

2. Discuss the nursing management of a patient in shock.

Nursing management begins with rapid assessment of airway, breathing and circulation. The nurse identifies the likely cause of shock and looks for life-threatening bleeding.

Vital signs, level of consciousness, peripheral perfusion and urine output are monitored closely.

The nurse provides oxygen when indicated, establishes or assists with appropriate vascular access, administers prescribed fluids and medications, and prepares for further emergency treatment.

The underlying cause must be addressed. The patient is continuously reassessed and deterioration is reported immediately.

3. Discuss the first aid and nursing management of a patient with a burn.

First ensure that the source of the burn is no longer causing injury and that the environment is safe.

Assess airway and breathing, particularly in patients with facial burns, inhalation concerns or extensive burns.

Assess the extent and depth of the burn and identify the mechanism. Significant burns require appropriate emergency assessment and may require referral to a specialized burn service.

Nursing care includes pain management, appropriate wound care, fluid management when indicated, infection prevention, nutritional support and psychological care.

4. Discuss the principles of disaster and mass-casualty nursing.

Disaster nursing begins with scene safety and activation of the appropriate emergency response system.

Triage is used to prioritize patients according to urgency while considering available resources.

Nurses work within established disaster plans, maintain communication with the multidisciplinary team and provide care within their scope of practice.

Accurate documentation, infection prevention, patient identification, psychological support and appropriate transfer are important components of disaster management.
