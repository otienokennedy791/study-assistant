Refractive errors occur when light is not focused correctly on the retina.

A cataract is an opacity of the lens that interferes with vision.

Common features: painless progressive visual impairment, blurred vision, glare and difficulty seeing at night.

Management: corrective lenses may help early symptoms, while surgery is the definitive treatment when vision is significantly affected.

Glaucoma refers to a group of eye disorders involving characteristic optic nerve damage, often associated with raised intraocular pressure.

Types: open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma.

Nursing priorities: medication administration, monitoring vision and symptoms, adherence education and urgent referral when acute angle-closure symptoms occur.

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva.

It may be infectious, allergic or associated with irritation.

Common findings include redness, discharge, irritation and watering.

Keratitis is inflammation of the cornea. A corneal ulcer is a defect involving loss of corneal epithelium with underlying inflammation or infection.

Symptoms may include pain, photophobia, redness, tearing and reduced vision.

These conditions require prompt ophthalmic assessment.

Uveitis is inflammation involving the uveal tract.

Possible features include eye pain, photophobia, redness and blurred vision.

Management depends on the underlying cause and may involve prescribed anti-inflammatory therapy.

Retinal detachment occurs when the retina separates from its normal supporting tissues.

Warning symptoms: flashes of light, new floaters and a curtain or shadow over the visual field.

This is an ophthalmic emergency requiring urgent assessment.

Diabetic retinopathy is retinal vascular damage associated with diabetes mellitus.

Good glucose and blood pressure control and regular eye screening are important in reducing the risk of vision loss.

Eye trauma may include blunt injury, penetrating injury, foreign bodies, chemical injuries and burns.

Assessment should determine the mechanism of injury, visual changes and associated injuries.

Chemical exposure to the eye requires immediate first aid.

The priority is prompt and prolonged irrigation according to the applicable emergency/ophthalmic protocol, followed by urgent professional assessment.

Do not delay irrigation while waiting for a detailed history.

  • Antibiotic eye preparations
  • Lubricating preparations
  • Anti-inflammatory medicines
  • Antiglaucoma medicines
  • Mydriatics and cycloplegics

Nurses should check the prescription, patient, eye, medication, dose and administration technique.

Preoperative care includes assessment, patient education, consent verification and preparation according to the procedure.

Postoperative care includes monitoring pain, vision, wound condition, infection and adherence to prescribed treatment.

Quick Revision Notes

Visual Acuity

Visual acuity measures the ability of the eye to see and distinguish details at a specified distance.

Cataract

Cataract is an opacity of the lens causing progressive visual impairment.

Glaucoma

Glaucoma involves characteristic optic nerve damage and visual field loss. Intraocular pressure may be elevated.

Retinal Detachment

Important warning symptoms include flashes, new floaters and a curtain-like shadow over the visual field.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy is a microvascular complication of diabetes affecting the retina.

Eye Trauma

Eye injuries can be serious even when external damage appears small. Mechanism and visual symptoms are important.

Chemical Injury

Immediate irrigation is a key emergency measure following chemical exposure to the eye.

Question Set 1

Short-Answer Questions

1. Define cataract.

Cataract is an opacity of the eye's lens that interferes with the passage and focusing of light, causing visual impairment.

2. State four symptoms of retinal detachment.

Flashes of light, new floaters, blurred or reduced vision, and a curtain or shadow over part of the visual field.

3. State four causes or risk factors for cataract.

Increasing age, diabetes, trauma, prolonged corticosteroid exposure, smoking and excessive ultraviolet exposure are recognized risk factors.

4. Differentiate myopia from hypermetropia.

Myopia causes distant objects to appear blurred because the image tends to focus in front of the retina. Hypermetropia causes difficulty with near vision because the image tends to focus behind the retina.

5. State four signs of conjunctivitis.

Eye redness, discharge, watering, irritation and itching may occur depending on the cause.

6. What is glaucoma?

Glaucoma is a group of eye disorders characterized by progressive optic nerve damage and corresponding visual field loss, often associated with elevated intraocular pressure.

7. State four eye assessment methods.

Visual acuity testing, pupil assessment, visual field assessment, eye movement assessment, tonometry and ophthalmoscopy.

8. State four nursing responsibilities when administering eye drops.

Verify the prescription and patient, perform hand hygiene, avoid contaminating the dropper tip, administer the correct number of drops and educate the patient as appropriate.

Long-Answer Questions

1. Discuss the nursing management of a patient with cataract.

Model answer:
  1. Assess visual impairment and its effect on activities of daily living.
  2. Assess the patient's understanding and concerns about treatment.
  3. Provide safety measures to reduce the risk of falls and injury.
  4. Prepare the patient for the planned procedure when surgery is indicated.
  5. Administer prescribed medications correctly.
  6. Provide postoperative monitoring and education.
  7. Teach the patient to report worsening pain, reduced vision, marked redness or other concerning symptoms promptly.
  8. Encourage follow-up according to the ophthalmic team's plan.

2. Discuss the nursing management of a patient with glaucoma.

Model answer:
  1. Assess vision and symptoms.
  2. Monitor the patient according to the prescribed ophthalmic assessment plan.
  3. Administer prescribed antiglaucoma medications correctly.
  4. Teach the patient the importance of adherence to treatment.
  5. Teach correct eye-drop technique.
  6. Encourage regular ophthalmic follow-up.
  7. Identify symptoms suggestive of acute angle-closure glaucoma, such as severe eye pain, blurred vision, halos, headache, nausea or vomiting, and facilitate urgent care.

3. Discuss the immediate nursing care of a patient with chemical eye injury.

Model answer:
  1. Recognize the injury as an emergency.
  2. Begin immediate irrigation according to the applicable emergency/ophthalmic protocol.
  3. Continue irrigation for the recommended duration while appropriate assessment and referral are arranged.
  4. Remove contact lenses if they can be removed safely during irrigation.
  5. Assess visual function when practical without delaying irrigation.
  6. Identify the chemical involved if possible, without delaying first aid.
  7. Arrange urgent ophthalmic assessment.
  8. Document the injury, first aid provided and the patient's response.

4. Discuss the nursing management of a patient with eye trauma.

Model answer:
  1. Assess the mechanism and time of injury.
  2. Assess visual symptoms and associated injuries.
  3. Avoid unnecessary pressure on a potentially injured globe.
  4. Protect the eye according to the suspected injury and local protocol.
  5. Do not attempt to remove an embedded object.
  6. Administer prescribed treatment.
  7. Arrange urgent ophthalmic assessment for serious injuries.
  8. Provide reassurance and patient education.
